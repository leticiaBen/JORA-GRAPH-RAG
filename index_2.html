Every industrial site in Algeria — a factory, a chemical plant, a construction yard — is bound by health, safety and environment (HSE) rules published in the Journal Officiel de la République Algérienne, the country's Official Gazette, known as JORA. The problem is that JORA is not a database. It's hundreds of PDF issues going back to independence, some digitized cleanly, many scanned from paper, none of it structured for search. If you're an HSE officer and you need to know whether a specific decree is still in force, or what it actually requires, your options are: read the gazette by hand, or already know the answer.

That's the problem I set out to fix during my final year. I built a system with two parts. The first automatically reads raw JORA PDFs and turns them into a knowledge graph — a database where, instead of rows and columns, information is stored as connected facts: this text cites that one, this body issued that decree, this article belongs to that domain. It's structured the way a lawyer actually thinks, not the way a stack of PDFs is stored. The second part is a conversational agent that sits on top of that graph, answering questions in plain French or Arabic, citing its sources, and — this mattered more to me than almost anything else — saying "I don't know" when the corpus genuinely doesn't cover a question.

The system is not a prototype gathering dust in a repo. It's deployed in production inside Regard, an HSE-tech platform built by Itihad Group, where real HSE and legal professionals query it today. This is the story of how it works, and what made it hard.

Why a knowledge graph, not just a vector search

The obvious first instinct for a system like this is what's usually called RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation): a common technique where, instead of trusting an AI model's memory, you have it search your own documents for relevant passages first, then answer based on those. In practice, that means cutting every document into small pieces, converting each piece into a list of numbers that captures its meaning (an "embedding"), and — when a question comes in — finding the pieces whose numbers are mathematically closest to the question's. That's not enough here on its own, and it's worth being specific about why. Legal answers in this corpus rarely live inside a single chunk of text. Whether a decree is still in force depends on tracing what abrogated or modified it — sometimes years later, in a completely different document. Whether a rule applies to a given installation depends on which body issued it and where that body sits in the legal hierarchy. None of that is captured by comparing meanings alone: two texts can read as very similar and be legally unrelated, or read as completely different topics and still be legally decisive — a one-line abrogation notice buried in an unrelated decree is exactly the kind of thing an embedding-based search misses, and a graph connection catches directly. A knowledge graph is what lets the system actually follow those relationships — CITES (cites), ABROGE (repeals), MODIFIES (amends), ISSUED_BY (issued by) — instead of hoping they happen to look similar in the text. That's the real argument for combining the two approaches (what's often called GraphRAG) here: the meaning-based search finds what's topically relevant, and the graph finds what's legally relevant — together they cover cases that neither would catch alone.

What makes the corpus itself hard has nothing to do with tooling and everything to do with how Algerian law is written and archived. JORA spans six decades of administrative French and Arabic, with formatting and drafting conventions drifting the whole way — a 1966 ordinance and a 2005 decree don't cite dates or institutions the same way — and roughly a third of the corpus exists only as scanned images with no text to extract at all. On top of that, legal texts don't stand alone: a decree can amend, repeal, or reference another, and a clause as small as "in view of law n°83-13" buried in a preamble is a real legal dependency — it determines which rule currently applies, and to whom. And no existing language model is built for Algerian administrative French or Arabic, so adapting one would have needed a large annotated dataset that simply doesn't exist. That combination is why the pipeline leans on a general-purpose vision-language model with careful prompting instead of a fine-tuned one, and why deduplication and citation resolution needed custom logic rather than a ready-made library.

The corpus: 376 issues, six decades, two formats

I worked from 376 JORA PDF files spanning 1966 to 2009, covering ten HSE domains — industrial safety, environmental protection, waste management, chemical and biological risks, occupational hygiene, fire protection, transport of dangerous goods, water and sanitation, and labor inspection. 239 of those documents (63.6%) had native, extractable text; the other 137 (36.4%) were scanned images with no text layer at all — some of them faxed decrees from the 1970s, barely legible even to a human reader.

Here's the part I want to be upfront about: given the time and LLM-API cost of processing legal text at this depth, I ran full extraction (EXTRACT) across all 376 documents, but entity extraction, graph construction, and embedding (TRANSFORM and LOAD) on a representative 118-document subset — 75 native and 43 scanned PDFs, chosen to cover all ten domains, both formats, every decade, and text lengths from two-article notices to 100-plus-article laws. The remaining 258 documents are already Markdown-ready for the same pipeline; extending coverage to the full corpus is a direct next step, not a redesign.

Turning PDFs into a graph

The pipeline runs in three phases. Extract pulls text out of every PDF. Documents that already contain digital text get parsed directly with PyMuPDF, a standard PDF-reading library. Scanned issues are just images with no text to copy, so they go through OCR (optical character recognition): a vision-language model (qwen3-vl-30b) reads the image and transcribes it, with Tesseract 5 — an older but reliable OCR engine — as a backup for French and Arabic when the model is unavailable. Transform takes that raw text and turns it into structured records — legal texts, articles, organizations, domains — each checked against a strict template (a Pydantic v2 schema) so a malformed or incomplete entry gets caught immediately instead of corrupting the graph later. It then resolves duplicate entities — the same decree can be referenced five different ways across five different documents — using a weighted score that combines entity type, number, year, and how close two titles are letter-by-letter (a standard text-similarity measure called Levenshtein distance). Load writes the result into Neo4j, a graph database, alongside a search index built from the same meaning-based embeddings described above, so the system can later search both by meaning and by graph connections.

Modeling the law as a graph

Concretely, the graph has five types of "things" (nodes) — legal texts, articles, organizations, domains, and text chunks — connected by nine types of labeled relationships, designed around the actual questions HSE officers ask: Has this decree been repealed? Which bodies issued texts on mining safety? What references this order?

Deduplication is where the messiness of real legal text shows up most — the same institution or decree gets written a different way almost every time it's mentioned. The system merges those duplicates automatically, but it's just as careful about when not to merge: genuinely distinct versions of the same law, like two different versions of the 1996 Constitution, are kept separate rather than incorrectly collapsed into one.

An agent that admits what it doesn't know

On top of the graph sits a conversational agent. Before answering, it first works out what kind of question it's dealing with: a simple factual lookup stays fast and cheap, while a question that clearly needs several sources triggers a deeper search. Under the hood, it combines the two retrieval methods described earlier — meaning-based search over text chunks, and graph traversal that literally walks the CITES and MENTIONS connections — so it can find both what's topically relevant and what's legally connected. A system prompt then enforces three hard rules: answer only from what was actually retrieved, always cite the source text and article, and say so explicitly when the corpus doesn't cover the question.

"What emergency plans are mandatory in high-risk zones?" — the agent pulled together four separate source texts (an ORSEC plan decree, a presidential decree, a workplace-hygiene decree, an evacuation order), structured the answer by theme, and closed with a note that no single transversal plan exists in Algerian law.

I spent a disproportionate amount of my evaluation time on the opposite case: questions the corpus can't answer. Watching the agent decline to answer, cite the absence of a matching source, and stop there — instead of confidently inventing a plausible-sounding decree — was the single result that made me trust the system enough to put it in front of real users.

What this leaves me with

What started as a way to make one gazette searchable turned into a broader question I keep coming back to: how much of the reasoning a lawyer does by hand — tracing citations, checking what's still in force, weighing which body has authority — can actually be captured in a graph instead of a person's memory. This project is my first real answer to that question, not the final one. The most direct next step is extending the graph from the 118-document subset to the full 376-document corpus, something the pipeline is already built to handle without a redesign. Beyond that, it's the question I'm carrying into my MSc: how to make structured reasoning over messy, real-world text more reliable, not just more powerful.
